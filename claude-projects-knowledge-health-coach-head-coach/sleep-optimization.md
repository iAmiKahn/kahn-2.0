# Sleep Optimization

If you suspect a sleep disorder (sleep apnea, chronic insomnia, restless leg syndrome), seek evaluation from a sleep specialist.

## Sleep Hygiene Fundamentals

### Why Sleep Matters for Body Recomposition
- Growth hormone (critical for muscle preservation and fat loss) is released primarily during deep sleep.
- Sleep deprivation increases cortisol, ghrelin (hunger hormone), and insulin resistance — directly opposing fat loss goals.
- Recovery from training happens during sleep, not during the workout.
- Chronic sleep restriction (<6 hours) shifts weight loss toward muscle loss rather than fat loss.

### The Non-Negotiable Basics
- **Consistent schedule:** Same wake time every day (including weekends) — within a 30-minute window.
- **Adequate duration:** 7-9 hours of sleep opportunity (time in bed). Most adults need 7.5-8.5 hours.
- **Dark room:** As close to pitch black as possible during sleep.
- **Cool room:** 65-68°F (18-20°C) is optimal for most people.
- **No screens 30-60 minutes before bed:** Or use blue-light blocking glasses as a minimum compromise.

## Building a Consistent Sleep Schedule

### How to Set Your Schedule
1. Choose a fixed wake time based on your obligations (this is the anchor — it does not move).
2. Count backward 8-8.5 hours — that is your target "lights out" time.
3. Add 30 minutes for wind-down before lights out — that is when you begin your pre-sleep routine.
4. Stick to this schedule 7 days per week. Weekend sleep-ins of more than 30 minutes disrupt your circadian rhythm.

### If Your Schedule Is Currently Chaotic
- Shift in 15-minute increments. Move bedtime earlier by 15 minutes every 2-3 days until you reach your target.
- Do not try to shift by more than 1 hour per week — the body resists rapid changes.
- Anchor the wake time FIRST (even if you're tired). The body will adjust sleep pressure to match within 1-2 weeks.

### Naps
- If fasting and energy dips hard mid-afternoon, a 20-minute nap (not longer) is acceptable.
- Nap before 2:00 PM to avoid disrupting nighttime sleep.
- Do not nap as a substitute for insufficient nighttime sleep — fix the root schedule.

## Environment Optimization

### Light
- **Morning:** Get bright light exposure within 30 minutes of waking (sunlight preferred; 10+ minutes). This sets your circadian clock.
- **Evening:** Dim lights 1-2 hours before bed. Overhead/bright lights signal "daytime" to your brain.
- **During sleep:** Pitch black. Use blackout curtains or a sleep mask. Cover LED lights on devices (electrical tape works).
- **If you wake at night:** Do not turn on bright lights. Use dim red/amber night lights if you need to navigate.

### Temperature
- **Room temperature:** 65-68°F (18-20°C). Cooler is generally better than warmer for sleep onset.
- **Body temperature:** A warm shower or bath 60-90 minutes before bed causes a rebound cooling effect that promotes sleep onset.
- **Extremities:** If feet are cold, wear socks to bed — cold extremities delay sleep onset.
- **Bedding:** Layer so you can adjust. Overheating causes awakenings.

### Noise
- **Ideal:** Quiet. If not possible, use consistent white/brown noise to mask intermittent sounds (fan, sound machine, app).
- **Avoid:** Falling asleep to TV, podcasts, or music with lyrics — it fragments sleep architecture even if you don't consciously wake.
- **Earplugs:** Acceptable if environmental noise is unavoidable. Silicone or foam.

### Bed Association
- Use the bed ONLY for sleep (and intimacy). No working, scrolling, or watching in bed.
- If you cannot sleep after 20 minutes, get up, go to another room, do something boring in dim light, and return when sleepy. This prevents the bed from becoming associated with wakefulness.

## What to Track and How to Report It

### Key Metrics (Simple Daily Tracking)
1. **Time in bed:** What time did you get in bed? What time did you get out?
2. **Estimated sleep duration:** Rough hours of actual sleep (subtract time awake).
3. **Sleep quality (1-5 scale):**
   - 1 = Terrible (multiple long awakenings, feel wrecked)
   - 2 = Poor (restless, woke often, feel unrested)
   - 3 = Okay (some awakenings, functional but not sharp)
   - 4 = Good (slept mostly through, feel rested)
   - 5 = Excellent (deep uninterrupted sleep, feel fully recovered)
4. **Number of wake-ups:** How many times did you wake during the night?
5. **Time to fall asleep (estimate):** <10 min / 10-20 min / 20-40 min / 40+ min
6. **Notes:** Anything unusual — stress, late meal, caffeine, alcohol, pain, noise, etc.

### Weekly Summary Pattern
- Average sleep quality score
- Average hours
- Number of nights below 7 hours
- Patterns (better/worse on certain days? After certain behaviors?)

### What These Numbers Tell You
- **Quality consistently below 3:** Something in environment, stress, or habits needs fixing.
- **Taking >30 min to fall asleep regularly:** Sleep drive may be insufficient (wake earlier, more activity) or anxiety/stimulation before bed.
- **Waking 3+ times per night:** Check temperature, noise, alcohol, bladder (reduce evening fluids), or stress.
- **Getting <7 hours consistently:** The schedule needs adjusting or sleep efficiency needs improving.

## How Sleep Interacts with Fasting and Exercise Recovery

### Sleep and Fasting
- **Extended fasts can disrupt sleep** — elevated cortisol, low blood sugar, and electrolyte shifts can cause insomnia or frequent waking. This is common on nights 2-3 of an extended fast.
- **Mitigation:** Take magnesium glycinate before bed during fasts (200-400 mg). Ensure sodium and potassium are adequate throughout the day. Accept that sleep may be lighter during extended fasts — this is temporary.
- **If insomnia is severe (two consecutive nights of very poor sleep during a fast):** This is a signal to break the fast. Sleep recovery is more important than continuing.
- **Time-restricted eating:** Avoid eating within 2-3 hours of bedtime. Late heavy meals (especially high-carb) disrupt sleep quality. However, going to bed extremely hungry also disrupts sleep. Find the window.
- **After breaking a long fast:** Sleep often improves dramatically on the first night of refeeding. The body prioritizes recovery.

### Sleep and Exercise Recovery
- **Muscle repair peaks during deep sleep** (stages 3-4, NREM). Without adequate sleep, training adaptations are blunted.
- **Overtraining + undersleeping = regression.** If sleep is consistently poor, reduce training volume until sleep normalizes.
- **Hard training increases sleep need.** After heavy training days, allow 8-9 hours of sleep opportunity.
- **Timing matters:** Intense exercise within 2-3 hours of bedtime can delay sleep onset (elevated core temp, cortisol, adrenaline). Train earlier if this is an issue. Low-intensity evening activity (walking, stretching, yoga) is fine or even helpful.
- **Rest days improve sleep architecture** — don't feel guilty about them.

## Common Sleep Disruptors and Practical Fixes

### Caffeine
- **Problem:** Caffeine has a half-life of 5-7 hours. A coffee at 2 PM still has half its caffeine active at 7-9 PM.
- **Fix:** Set a hard caffeine cutoff. Typical starting point: no caffeine after 12:00 PM (noon). If sleep is still poor, try 10:00 AM. This applies to coffee, tea, pre-workout, energy drinks, and dark chocolate.
- **During fasts:** Caffeine sensitivity increases because it's absorbed faster on an empty stomach. Lower your dose during fasts (half your normal amount).

### Screens and Blue Light
- **Problem:** Blue light from phones, tablets, and monitors suppresses melatonin production and delays sleep onset.
- **Fix:** No screens 30-60 minutes before bed. If unavoidable, use blue-light blocking glasses (amber/orange lenses) AND enable night mode on devices. Neither alone is sufficient — the combination helps.
- **Better alternative:** Replace screen time with: reading a physical book, stretching, journaling, conversation, or a non-stimulating audio (ambient music, sleep story).

### Alcohol
- **Problem:** Alcohol helps you fall asleep faster but destroys sleep architecture — suppresses REM sleep, causes awakenings in the second half of the night, and dehydrates.
- **Fix:** Avoid alcohol within 3 hours of bedtime. If drinking, limit quantity and hydrate before sleep. Best option: eliminate alcohol entirely during body recomposition phases.

### Stress and Racing Thoughts
- **Problem:** Cortisol spike from unresolved stress or anxiety keeps the brain in "alert" mode.
- **Fixes:**
  - Brain dump: Write tomorrow's tasks on paper BEFORE the wind-down period. Getting them out of your head reduces rumination.
  - Box breathing: Inhale 4 seconds → hold 4 → exhale 4 → hold 4. Repeat 5-10 rounds.
  - Progressive muscle relaxation: Tense each muscle group for 5 seconds, then release. Start at feet, work up to face.
  - If a specific problem is causing anxiety: write it down and explicitly defer it to tomorrow. Give yourself permission to stop solving it tonight.

### Late Eating
- **Problem:** Eating within 2 hours of bed — especially large, high-carb, or high-fat meals — elevates core temperature and triggers digestion, reducing sleep quality.
- **Fix:** Finish last meal 2-3 hours before bed. If genuinely hungry before bed, a small protein-rich snack (handful of nuts, small Greek yogurt) is better than a large meal or going to bed so hungry you can't sleep.

### Irregular Schedule
- **Problem:** Varying your bed/wake times by more than 1 hour day-to-day creates "social jet lag" — your circadian rhythm never locks in.
- **Fix:** Choose a fixed wake time and do not deviate by more than 30 minutes, even on weekends. Your body will begin to naturally feel sleepy at the appropriate time within 1-2 weeks.

### Environmental Disruptions (Partner, Pets, Noise)
- **Problem:** External disturbances fragment sleep even if you fall back asleep quickly. Each waking resets your sleep cycle progress.
- **Fixes:**
  - White/brown noise machine to mask variable sounds.
  - Separate blankets if partner moves or runs different temperature.
  - Pets: train them out of the bedroom, or confine to a specific area that doesn't disturb you.
  - If partner snores: earplugs, white noise, or encourage them to seek evaluation for sleep apnea.
